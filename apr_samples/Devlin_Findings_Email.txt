Here are the key points of our analysis:

• An implied warranty against defective workmanship is a legal concept that applies to construction. It is a warranty that is not explicitly stated but is implied by the law. Essentially, it means that construction on a home must be completed to a certain standard of workmanship, and that the contractor is responsible for any defects or issues that result from poor workmanship. Florida law provides a basic framework governing construction defects, set forth in Florida Statutes Chapter 558.
• The statutes also provide that a contractor must be given a notice and opportunity to repair the construction defects. The claimant must give at least 60 days written notice to the contractor if the defect arises from work performed under a contract.
• If such notice is provided to the contractor, the contractor has 45 days to provide a written response that provides a detailed description of the proposed repairs necessary to remedy the defect and a timetable of the repairs.
• An action cannot be filed against the contractor without first complying with the notice and opportunity to repair requirements.
• You may also have a breach of contract claim against the contractor. To prove a breach of contract, you must prove the following: (1) a valid contract; (2) a material breach; and (3) damages. See Murciano v. Garcia, 958 So. 2d 423, 423 (Fla. 3d DCA 2007). Presumably, the Contract includes an obligation of good faith as well as express warranties. It is undisputed that the Parties entered into an enforceable Contract, which may have been breached when the contractor failed to complete the project in the manner set forth in the specifications you were given before construction began and as required by industry standards. In addition, the issues set forth in detail above all appear to fall within either express or implied warranty coverage, and thus contractor’s failure to repair and replace these issues and complete the project could lead to a breach of contract cause of action, entitling you to damages and/or remedies under the Contract and Florida law
• On the issue of liens, in Florida, general contractors and subcontractors have the right to file a mechanic’s lien against the property where they performed work and for which they were not paid. Pursuant to Florida Statute § 713.02 (4), “Persons who are not in privity with an owner and who perform labor or services or furnish materials constituting a part of an improvement under the direct contract of another person shall have rights to lien on real property as provided in s. 713.06.”
• Please note that failure to make full payment pursuant to the terms of a construction contract affords certain rights to the Contractor and subcontractors, which may include the ability to record a lien against the property for the unpaid balance and/or to void warranties if provided by the terms of the contract. Additionally, the Contractor or subcontractors may attempt to recover attorneys’ fees in connection with its effort to collect the unpaid balance.
• You have received a Notice to Owner, which is the first required step to file a lien which could result in the imminent recording of a lien against your property.

Based on the above, a negotiated resolution would likely be your most efficient and cost-effective path forward. One option would be to issue a demand letter to the contractor demanding that contractor complete the project in accordance with the terms of the contract or reimburse any amounts you may be required to pay associated with hiring another contractor to complete the project. This may lead to a joint resolution that includes mutual waivers and a clear release of future liability. Furthermore, you should reach out to the subcontractor and pay the amount owed under the Notice to Owner to avoid a lien being placed on your home. You can seek reimbursement of the amount paid from the contractor as a result of his failure to pay subcontractors.
