IV. Governing Law

Jurisdiction Florida (Hillsborough County)

Frameworks Florida contract law; Florida construction licensing statutes (Fla. Stat. ch. 489); Florida mechanics’ lien law (Fla. Stat. ch. 713, pt. I); FDUTPA (Fla. Stat. §§ 501.201–501.213)

A. Breach of Contract and Damages

  • Elements: a valid contract, a material breach, and damages. See Friedman v. N.Y. Life Ins. Co., 985 So. 2d 56, 58 (Fla. 4th DCA 2008).
  • Measure of damages in construction: generally the reasonable cost of remedying defects and completing the work, unless economic waste would result. See Grossman Holdings Ltd. v. Hourihan, 414 So. 2d 1037, 1039–40 (Fla. 1982).
  • Substantial performance doctrines may affect offsets; defective work typically entitles owner to deduct cost to cure. See, e.g., Ocean Ridge Dev. Corp. v. Quality Plastering, Inc., 247 So. 2d 72 (Fla. 4th DCA 1971).

B. Implied Duty of Workmanlike Performance / Warranties

  • Florida recognizes an implied duty that contractors perform in a reasonably workmanlike manner consistent with industry standards when in privity with the owner. See, e.g., Lochrane Eng’g, Inc. v. Willingham Realgrowth Inv. Fund, Ltd., 552 So. 2d 228, 232 (Fla. 5th DCA 1989).
  • Where defects affect habitability or essential services, remedies may also implicate warranty concepts addressed in Maronda Homes, Inc. v. Lakeview Reserve HOA, 127 So. 3d 1258 (Fla. 2013).

C. Contractor Licensing and Permitting

  • Chapter 489 regulates contractor licensing. An unlicensed contractor may not enforce a contract and is barred from lien rights. See Fla. Stat. § 489.128(1).
  • Unlicensed contracting is a prohibited act subject to penalties. See Fla. Stat. § 489.127; § 489.13.
  • Permitting and code compliance are material to performance; lack of permits can evidence breach and expose the contractor to sanctions.

D. Florida Mechanics’ Lien Law (Chapter 713)

  • Notice to Owner (NTO): Most subs/suppliers must serve an NTO within 45 days of commencing work to preserve lien rights. Fla. Stat. § 713.06(2)(a).
  • Claim of Lien: Must be recorded within 90 days of final furnishing. Fla. Stat. § 713.08(5).
  • Duration/Foreclosure: Lien expires after 1 year unless suit is filed; owner may shorten to 60 days with a Notice of Contest. Fla. Stat. § 713.22(1)–(2).
  • Owner protections: Owner should obtain proper conditional/unconditional lien releases upon each payment. Fla. Stat. § 713.20; “improper payments” after receiving NTOs, without securing releases, may not reduce lien exposure. See Fla. Stat. § 713.06(3)(c), (d).
  • Sworn Statement of Account: Owner may demand detailed accounting from lienors. Fla. Stat. § 713.16(2).
  • Discharge/Transfer: Liens may be discharged or transferred to bond. Fla. Stat. §§ 713.21, 713.24.
  • Attorney’s fees: Prevailing party fees in lien actions. Fla. Stat. § 713.29.

E. FDUTPA (Deceptive/Unfair Trade Practices)

  • Prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in trade or commerce. Fla. Stat. § 501.204(1).
  • Private actions: Fla. Stat. § 501.211; prevailing party fees: § 501.2105.
  • Misrepresentations about material facts (e.g., claiming cabinets were purchased/stored when not) may constitute a deceptive act if they are likely to mislead a reasonable consumer and cause actual damages. See Rollins, Inc. v. Butland, 951 So. 2d 860, 869–70 (Fla. 2d DCA 2006).

F. Limitations and Repose

  • Written contract: five-year limitations period. Fla. Stat. § 95.11(2)(b).
  • Construction defect: four-year statute of limitations with discovery rules for latent defects; seven-year statute of repose (as amended 2023). See Fla. Stat. § 95.11(3)(c).

V. Legal Analysis Applied to Your Facts

1. Breach of Contract / Defective and Incomplete Work

  • Alleged deficiencies (e.g., bathroom redo, workmanship issues) and incomplete items support a claim that LLW materially breached by failing to perform in a workmanlike manner and to complete the agreed scope.
  • Damages: cost to repair defects and complete unfinished work, reasonably necessary and proven via expert estimates, less any unpaid contract balance. See Grossman Holdings, 414 So. 2d at 1039–40.
  • If the contract contains notice-and-cure clauses, those should be followed to preserve claims and avoid defenses based on failure to allow an opportunity to cure.

2. Misrepresentations Regarding Cabinets (FDUTPA / Breach)

  • Representations that cabinets had been purchased/stored, followed by your out-of-pocket purchase, raise potential deceptive act and misrepresentation issues under FDUTPA and a breach of contract/warranty theory if those cabinets were part of the contract scope.
  • Damages: actual damages may include reimbursement of the amounts you paid due to the misrepresentation, as well as repair/installation costs, subject to any contract limitations.

3. Subcontractor Nonpayment and Lien Exposure

  • Receipt of a notice containing lien verbiage likely indicates an NTO. If a proper NTO was served, the subcontractor may perfect a mechanic’s lien by timely recording a claim of lien.
  • Payments made to LLW after receipt of an NTO, without obtaining proper lien releases, can be deemed “improper payments,” potentially exposing the property to liens regardless of sums already paid. Fla. Stat. § 713.06(3)(c).
  • Owner tools: demand sworn statements of account (§ 713.16); require contractor’s final payment affidavit (§ 713.06(3)(d)); withhold further payments until proper releases are provided (§§ 713.06, 713.20).

4. Licensing/Permitting

  • If LLW or Mr. Winiarek were unlicensed for the category of work performed, LLW’s ability to enforce contract rights and lien rights may be barred (§ 489.128), strengthening your negotiating position and potential defenses to any lien claims asserted by the prime contractor.
  • Permit irregularities can constitute evidence of breach and may support agency complaints and administrative remedies.

5. Alternative and Supplemental Claims

  • Unjust enrichment/quantum meruit (alternative pleading): if the contract is rescinded or deemed unenforceable, you may seek restitution of payments not matched by value received.
  • Breach of warranty: defective workmanship supports a warranty-based claim where applicable.
  • Civil theft or fraud: potentially implicated by egregious misappropriation of funds, but these claims require heightened proof and a pre-suit demand for civil theft (Fla. Stat. § 772.11). We recommend reserving evaluation pending further evidence.

VI. Damages and Remedies

  • Compensatory damages: cost to properly repair and complete work; reimbursement of amounts paid for items misrepresented or not delivered (e.g., cabinets); potential diminution in value only if cost-to-repair is impracticable.
  • Rescission/restitution: where appropriate and strategically advantageous, particularly if licensing issues are present.
  • Statutory fees: if pursuing FDUTPA (§ 501.2105) or defending/enforcing lien rights (§ 713.29). Contractual fee-shifting may also apply.
  • Lien relief: discharge, Notice of Contest to shorten enforcement period, or transfer to bond to clear title. See Fla. Stat. §§ 713.21, 713.22(2), 713.24.
  • Administrative remedies: complaints to DBPR for licensing violations; potential insurance claims against contractor’s CGL policy if property damage from defective work is implicated.
