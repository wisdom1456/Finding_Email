4) Legal Analysis

4.1 Breach of Contract

  • Elements: (1) valid contract; (2) material breach; (3) damages. See Abbott Labs., Inc. v. General Electric Capital, 765 So. 2d 737, 740 (Fla. 5th DCA 2000).
  • On the reported facts, failure to complete the scope and performance of defective work (e.g., bathroom redo) supports a material breach claim and damages for cost of completion and repair [1].
  • Measure of damages in construction cases is typically cost to repair/complete, unless that would result in economic waste. See Grossman Holdings Ltd. v. Hourihan, 414 So. 2d 1037 (Fla. 1982).
  • If the contract includes notice-and-cure or dispute resolution clauses (mediation/arbitration), those provisions must be reviewed and followed to preserve rights.

4.2 Construction Defects – Chapter 558 Pre-suit

  • Before filing a lawsuit alleging construction defects, Florida requires service of a Chapter 558 notice of claim outlining the defects and allowing the contractor an opportunity to inspect and propose a remedy (§ 558.004, Fla. Stat.), unless the contract opts out.
  • Advantages: creates a record, may prompt settlement or repair proposals, and preserves evidence. It does not preclude concurrent lien defense steps.

4.3 Lien Exposure and Owner Protections — Chapter 713

  • Subcontractor “Notice to Owner” (NTO) is generally due within 45 days of first furnishing labor/materials (§ 713.06(2)(a)); a claim of lien must be recorded within 90 days after final furnishing and served on you (§ 713.08).
  • Owner Tools:
    • Demand for Sworn Statement of Account to lienor (§ 713.16(2)).
    • Demand list of all subs/suppliers from contractor (§ 713.165).
    • Require and collect conditional/unconditional lien waivers with each draw (§ 713.20).
    • Withhold final payment absent contractor’s affidavit (§ 713.06(3)(d)).
    • If a lien is recorded, shorten the foreclosure deadline via Notice of Contest of Lien (60 days) (§ 713.22(2)) or by transferring the lien to a bond (§ 713.24) or by “show cause” proceedings (20 days) (§ 713.21).
  • Prevailing party fees are available in lien actions (§ 713.29).
  • Reported subcontractor nonpayment and NTOs heighten urgency to implement these measures [1].

4.4 Licensing and Consumer Protection

  • If LLW or Mr. Winiarek is unlicensed or violates licensing rules, administrative penalties and consumer remedies may apply (§§ 489.127, 489.129).
  • For residential jobs, Florida restricts certain payment practices and provides consumer protections regarding contractor use of funds (§ 489.126), and misapplication of construction funds may implicate § 713.345.
  • FDUTPA prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices (§ 501.204); private remedies include actual damages and potential attorneys’ fees (§§ 501.211, 501.2105). Misrepresentations related to the cabinets and project status, if proven, may support FDUTPA claims [1].

4.5 Prompt Pay and Draw Administration

  • Florida’s private prompt pay laws can affect payment timing and interest for wrongfully withheld or delayed payments (§§ 715.12–715.13), but your priority now is to prevent improper payments that could increase lien exposure and to document any setoffs for corrective work.
  • Bank inspections may be relevant to documenting progress and payment appropriateness; obtain full copies for the file [1].

5) Risk Assessment

  • Lien risk: Subcontractor notices indicate potential claims of lien that could cloud title and affect refinancing/sale. Immediate monitoring of county records and proactive responses are advised [1].
  • Scope and cost risk: Defective/incomplete work may require significant demolition and rework (e.g., bathroom), increasing cost-to-complete and delay damages [1].
  • Counterclaims/defenses: Contractor may allege owner interference or nonpayment; robust documentation and expert evaluation will be critical.
  • Licensing exposure: If licensing violations exist, they can bolster leverage, affect enforceability of lien/contract, and support administrative complaints.

6) Recommendations and Next Steps

6.1 Immediate Protective Actions (0–10 days)

  • Cease any non-required payments to LLW until and unless proper conditional lien waivers are furnished from LLW and all known subs/suppliers corresponding to each prior draw (§ 713.20).
  • Send a written demand to LLW for:
    • A current list of all subcontractors/suppliers and their payment status (§ 713.165).
    • A contractor’s affidavit if any final/next draw is requested (§ 713.06(3)(d)).
    • Proof of purchase, delivery, and storage for the cabinets and any other materials claimed pre-purchased (invoices, bills of lading, storage receipts) [1].
  • Serve Chapter 713 sworn statement of account demands on any lienor or potential lienor that served an NTO (§ 713.16(2)).
  • Begin daily photographic/video documentation of site conditions; preserve all texts/emails, bank inspection reports, and draw requests [1].
  • Order an independent licensed inspector or construction expert to assess defects, code compliance, and provide a cost-to-complete/repair report.
  • Monitor the county official records weekly for any recorded claims of lien; if filed, consider recording a Notice of Contest to shorten the foreclosure window to 60 days (§ 713.22(2)), or transferring to bond (§ 713.24) if strategically advisable.

6.2 Contractual Enforcement and Cure Process (10–30 days)

  • Issue a formal Notice of Default and Demand to Cure to LLW, identifying specific defective/incomplete items, with a reasonable cure period consistent with the contract and Chapter 558 pre-suit process (§ 558.004), unless your contract has opted out.
  • If the contract requires mediation/arbitration, calendar and initiate those processes promptly to avoid waiver of contractual remedies.
  • Evaluate grounds and timing for termination for cause, ensuring strict compliance with contractual notice and opportunity to cure provisions to preserve claims and mitigate wrongful termination exposure.

6.3 Claims Strategy (30–60 days)

  • Prepare Chapter 558 Notice of Claim with expert support, detailing each defect and demanded remedies (§ 558.004).
  • Pursue breach of contract damages for cost to repair/complete, delay-related costs, and reimbursement of amounts paid for unperformed or defective work [1]; include unjust enrichment as an alternative theory.
  • Assess FDUTPA claim viability regarding alleged misrepresentations (e.g., cabinets) and any deceptive practices; seek attorney’s fees under § 501.2105 as appropriate [1].
  • File administrative complaints with DBPR (CILB) for licensing violations and with appropriate consumer agencies if supported by evidence (§§ 489.127, 489.129).
  • If liens are recorded, assert statutory defenses, demand strict compliance, and consider fee shifting under § 713.29.

6.4 Financial Recovery and Mitigation

  • Obtain a detailed, independent cost-to-complete and correction estimate to quantify damages for negotiation, mediation, or litigation.
  • Evaluate setoff against any remaining contract balance and consider use of joint checks for substantiated unpaid subs to mitigate lien risk, conforming to proper payment rules in § 713.06.
  • If LLW is properly licensed and you obtain a qualifying judgment, evaluate a claim under the Homeowners’ Construction Recovery Fund (§ 489.141).

Urgent: Because subcontractors have already issued lien-related notices, time-sensitive statutory steps (e.g., demands for sworn statements, notices of contest) and evidence preservation should be prioritized immediately [1].
