Legal Analysis

1) Breach of Contract

  • Material breach: Failure to perform per plans/specifications, to code, and within agreed timelines typically constitutes a material breach under Florida law.
  • Damages: Recoverable damages generally include the reasonable cost to repair/replace defective work and the cost to complete unfinished work, plus consequential damages permitted by the contract and prejudgment interest.
  • Termination: If the contractor materially breached, you may terminate for cause pursuant to the contract. Florida law also recognizes termination for material breach, subject to compliance with any contractual notice-and-cure requirements to avoid allegations of wrongful termination.
  • Contract terms to review: Notice and cure provisions; warranty clauses; retainage; dispute resolution (mediation/arbitration clauses); attorney’s fee shifting; venue; change-order requirements; payment milestones tied to inspection approvals.

2) Defective/Substandard Work and Code Compliance

  • Standard of care: Contractors must perform in a good and workmanlike manner and comply with the Florida Building Code and applicable permits/inspections.
  • Proof: Independent expert inspection and a detailed scope-of-repair/cost-to-complete report are critical. Photographing, documenting, and preserving defective conditions is essential.
  • Chapter 558: Before filing a civil action for construction defects, Florida’s Chapter 558 generally requires serving a pre-suit notice describing the defects and allowing an opportunity to inspect and offer to cure. We will tailor the notice to preserve remedies without waiving rights.

3) Mechanics’ Lien Exposure and Owner Protections (Chapter 713)

  • Notice of Commencement (§713.13): Confirm whether a Notice of Commencement was recorded and posted. Its proper use governs lien rights and owner payment protections.
  • Notices to Owner and Claims of Lien (§§713.06, 713.08):
    • Subcontractors lacking privity must timely serve a Notice to Owner (generally within 45 days of commencing work and prior to final payment).
    • Claim of Lien must be recorded within 90 days of last furnishing and enforced within 1 year unless shortened by owner action.
  • Owner tools:
    • Demand for Sworn Statement of Account (§713.16) to any lienor, compelling detailed accounting.
    • Contractor’s Final Payment Affidavit (typically under §713.06(3)) before final payment to ensure subs are paid.
    • Releases/Waivers (§713.20) at each payment; request conditional releases if appropriate.
    • Notice of Contest of Lien (§713.22(2)) to shorten enforcement to 60 days, or a Show Cause procedure (§713.21) potentially reducing to 20 days.
    • Transfer of lien to bond (§713.24) as needed to clear title for financing/sale.
    • Notice of Termination (§713.132), where appropriate, to terminate the Notice of Commencement after proper protections.
  • Misapplication of construction funds (§713.345): A contractor’s diversion of owner payments intended for subs/suppliers can constitute a criminal offense. While criminal remedies are separate, this statute provides leverage and context for complaints to regulators.

4) Nonpayment to Subcontractors; Owner Double-Payment Risk

  • If subs were not paid, they may assert lien rights against the property even though you paid the contractor. Owner protections depend on timely receipt of Notices to Owner and your adherence to statutory payment procedures and release collection.
  • We will evaluate whether your payments were protected (i.e., conditioned on proper releases) and whether any additional owner defenses apply due to contractor noncompliance.

5) Licensing and Consumer Protection

  • Licensing (Chapter 489):
    • If LLW Construction or Mr. Winiarek was unlicensed for the scope, §489.128 may render the contract unenforceable by the contractor, and §489.127 addresses unlicensed activity penalties.
    • Unlicensed contracting may also support claims for restitution and attorney’s fees under other statutes (e.g., §768.0425(2) addresses attorney’s fees for damages caused by an unlicensed contractor).
  • FDUTPA (Chapter 501, Part II):
    • Unfair or deceptive acts, such as misrepresentations about licensing, quality, or the status of payments to subs, can support FDUTPA claims (§501.204).
    • Remedies include actual damages and potential attorney’s fees (§501.211, §501.2105). We will tailor the measure of damages to include cost to repair/complete and other losses permitted by law.

6) Litigation and Dispute Resolution Posture

  • Pre-suit: Chapter 558 notice for defects; demand for accounting and releases under Chapter 713; formal notice of default and opportunity to cure per the contract.
  • Mediation/Arbitration: Many construction contracts require mediation and/or arbitration. We will confirm and proceed accordingly.
  • Civil claims: Potential counts include breach of contract; breach of warranty/workmanlike performance; FDUTPA; statutory claims under Chapter 489; unjust enrichment in the alternative; and, where appropriate, conversion/misapplication theories tied to §713.345 facts.
  • Venue: Hillsborough County Circuit Court for claims exceeding $50,000, absent a binding arbitration provision.

Risk Assessment

  • Lien risk: Moderate to high if subs remain unpaid; mitigable through §713.16 demands, obtaining releases, and, if liens are filed, using §713.22(2) to shorten enforcement or §713.24 to bond off.
  • Defective work proof: High likelihood of establishing breach if expert inspection corroborates deficiencies and code violations.
  • Counterclaims: Expect potential counterclaims for wrongful termination or unpaid contract balance; compliance with contract notice/cure provisions is critical.
  • Licensing/FDUTPA: If licensing or deceptive practices issues are substantiated, your leverage and fee-shifting prospects improve materially.

Recommendations and Immediate Next Steps

  • Commission a licensed, independent construction expert to inspect, document, and report on defects and incomplete work; include photos, measurements, code citations, and a cost-to-complete/repair estimate.
  • Secure and backup all communications, plans/specs, permits/inspections, change orders, payment applications, and lien/release documents.

B. Lien Exposure Management (Chapter 713)

  • Confirm whether a Notice of Commencement (§713.13) was recorded and posted; obtain a certified copy.
  • Issue Demands for Sworn Statement of Account (§713.16) to any known lienors/subcontractors and to LLW Construction, compelling itemized amounts due/paid, dates, and retained sums.
  • Require and collect conditional/progress and final lien releases (§713.20) from LLW and all subs/suppliers corresponding to each payment made to date.
  • If any Claim of Lien is recorded, consider filing a Notice of Contest (§713.22(2)) to shorten the enforcement period to 60 days or initiating show cause (§713.21) to reduce it further, after strategic review.
  • Evaluate bonding off liens (§713.24) if needed to protect marketability or financing.

C. Contractor Default, Defect, and Cure Process

  • Serve a formal Notice of Default and Demand to Cure per contract, identifying specific breaches, defective work, and deadlines, and reserving all rights. Include a demand for verified proof of payment to subs.
  • Prepare and serve the Chapter 558 Notice of Construction Defect, detailing defects and providing inspection access and a deadline for any offer to cure.
  • Concurrently, suspend further payments pending delivery of required releases and compliance, consistent with §713.06 practices and contract terms.

D. Regulatory and Consumer Protection Leverage

  • Verify LLW Construction and Mr. Winiarek’s licensure status for the scope at issue with the Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR).
  • If warranted, file complaints with DBPR (Chapter 489 issues), the local building department, and, for deceptive practices, consider a FDUTPA claim and complaint to the Florida Attorney General’s consumer protection division.

E. Litigation/Mediation/Arbitration Strategy

  • After Chapter 558 timelines run (or earlier as permitted), initiate mediation. If unsuccessful, file suit or arbitration per the dispute resolution clause.
  • Primary remedies sought:
    • Reimbursement of the approximately $100,000 paid for incomplete/defective work.
    • Cost to repair/replace defective work and cost to complete the project with a new contractor.
    • Fee shifting and costs where authorized (contract, §501.2105 FDUTPA, §768.0425(2) for unlicensed contractor damages).
    • Equitable relief including rescission or disgorgement, where appropriate.
    • Alternative: specific performance to complete to code and standard, with bonding and inspection safeguards.
  • If material breach is established and contractual requirements are satisfied, proceed with termination for cause and secure a replacement contractor after safeguarding against lien exposure.
