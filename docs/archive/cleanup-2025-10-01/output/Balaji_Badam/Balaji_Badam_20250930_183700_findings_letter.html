Applicable Law

  • Nonpayment and Termination/Notice: Fla. Stat. § 83.56(3) (3 business days to cure nonpayment, excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays).
  • Waiver by Acceptance of Rent: Fla. Stat. § 83.56(5) (acceptance of partial rent after notice can waive eviction rights unless statutory safeguards are followed).
  • Holdover Remedies: Fla. Stat. § 83.58 (double rent for tenant holding over without permission after lease expiration) and § 83.59 (action for possession).
  • Attorney’s Fees: Fla. Stat. § 83.48 (prevailing party fees if the lease provides for fees).
  • Security Deposits: Fla. Stat. § 83.49(3)(a)–(b) (30-day notice to impose a claim against the deposit; 15 days to return if no claim; notice to last known address).
  • Abandoned Personal Property: Fla. Stat. ch. 715 (e.g., §§ 715.10–715.111) (statutory notice and disposition procedures for property left behind by the tenant).
  • Collections Compliance: Florida Consumer Collection Practices Act, Fla. Stat. §§ 559.55–559.785 (collection communications standards).
  • Liquidated Damages and Penalties: Florida contract law enforces reasonable liquidated damages and disfavors penalties; see, e.g., Lefemine v. Baron, 573 So. 2d 326 (Fla. 1991).
  • Venue/Amount in Controversy: Small claims jurisdiction up to $8,000 (exclusive of costs/fees); landlord–tenant actions proceed in county court.

Analysis

1) Unpaid Rent and Late Fees

  • Breach: The tenant’s failure to pay rent in full and on time constitutes a material breach of the lease. You may recover unpaid rent and any lease-authorized late fees that are reasonable and not unconscionable under Florida contract law.
  • Notice to Pay or Vacate: If the tenant remains in possession, a written 3-business-day notice under Fla. Stat. § 83.56(3) is required before filing for eviction for nonpayment. The 3 days exclude weekends and legal holidays. Service should comply with statute and lease provisions (e.g., hand delivery, posting at the premises, and mailing as applicable).
  • Partial Payments and Waiver: Acceptance of rent (or partial rent) after service of a 3-day notice can waive the right to evict unless you follow the safeguards in § 83.56(5), such as issuing a compliant receipt that reserves rights or depositing the partial payment into the court registry when filing the eviction.
  • Damages: On the provided figures, the outstanding balance of rent and late fees is $4,505. Maintain a detailed ledger and proof of each payment/charge to support a damages claim. Prejudgment interest may be available on liquidated sums under Florida law, and post-judgment interest accrues at the statutory rate (see Fla. Stat. § 55.03 for the rate).

2) Administrative Fee ($1,000)

  • Contract Basis Required: Recovery depends on clear lease authorization. If the lease expressly provides for a specific administrative/default fee triggered by nonpayment or breach, it is more likely to be enforceable.
  • Liquidated Damages vs. Penalty: Under Florida law, a liquidated damages clause is enforceable if actual damages are difficult to ascertain at contract formation and the amount is not grossly disproportionate to the anticipated loss (Lefemine v. Baron). If the $1,000 fee is not tied to actual administrative costs or is disproportionate, a court may deem it an unenforceable penalty.
  • Recommendation: Include the fee in the demand if supported by the lease, but be prepared to negotiate or to proceed for $4,505 if the fee’s enforceability is challenged.

3) Keys, Possession, and Potential Holdover

  • Possession Status: The tenant reportedly intended to vacate on June 13 but did not return keys. Failure to return keys complicates confirmation of surrender. If the tenant remains in possession, you must proceed through formal eviction and cannot self-help (see Fla. Stat. § 83.67 prohibiting lockouts and utility shutoffs).
  • Holdover: If the lease expired and the tenant held over without consent, you may seek possession and double rent for the holdover period under Fla. Stat. § 83.58, calculated on a daily basis until surrender.
  • Abandoned Property: If the tenant has vacated but left personal property, follow Fla. Stat. ch. 715 notice procedures before disposing of or selling property. The statute requires giving written notice and a reasonable time window for retrieval (generally at least 10 days if personally delivered or 15 days if mailed).
  • Security Deposit: If you intend to claim against any security deposit, serve the 30-day notice of intent to impose a claim to the tenant’s last known address as required by Fla. Stat. § 83.49(3).

4) Litigation and Collection Pathways

  • Eviction + Damages: If the tenant remains in possession and does not cure after a proper 3-day notice, file a county court action for eviction (possession) and related monetary damages (unpaid rent/fees). Eviction counts receive priority, followed by adjudication of damages.
  • Small Claims for Money Only: If the tenant has vacated, file a small claims action in Duval County for the outstanding balance. The $5,505 sought falls within small claims jurisdiction. You may recover court costs and, if the lease authorizes, reasonable attorney’s fees (Fla. Stat. § 83.48).
  • Collections Compliance: Any demand communications should comply with the Florida Consumer Collection Practices Act (Fla. Stat. §§ 559.55–559.785). Avoid deceptive or harassing conduct, and state accurate amounts and bases for the debt.

Recommendations and Next Steps

Immediate Actions (0–5 days)

  • Issue Demand Letter: Send a written demand to Breanna Herring for $5,505 with a firm deadline (e.g., 10 calendar days) and detailed payoff instructions. Send via certified mail to the property and any last known forwarding address, and by email/text if permitted by the lease. Itemize rent, late fees, and the administrative fee (with lease clause citations).
  • Confirm Possession: Attempt to schedule a keys return. If no response, document the property condition from the exterior and any reliable indications of vacancy (without breaching the peace). Do not enter or change locks unless you have clear legal possession or a court order.
  • Prepare Required Notices: If the tenant may still be in possession and unpaid, prepare and serve a 3-business-day notice under § 83.56(3). Track business days only (excluding weekends/legal holidays). Retain proof of service.
  • Ledger and Documentation: Finalize a detailed rent ledger, lease copy, addenda, late fee clauses, any admin fee clauses, communications, and proof of payments.

If No Payment by Deadline (5–15 days)

  • File Suit:
    • If possession is still at issue: file an eviction action (count for possession) and a separate count for damages (unpaid rent, late fees, and administrative fee if supported).
    • If possession is no longer at issue: file a small claims action for $5,505 (or $4,505 if electing to omit the administrative fee) plus costs, contractual attorney’s fees (if provided), and applicable interest.
  • Security Deposit Compliance: If applicable, within 30 days of tenant’s vacate date, send the § 83.49 notice of intent to impose a claim against the deposit to the last known address by certified mail.
  • Personal Property: If items are left, send Chapter 715 abandoned property notice, specifying pickup deadline and storage charges if any. Proceed with disposition only after the statutory period expires.

Risk and Strategy Considerations

  • Administrative Fee: Collectability hinges on the lease language and reasonableness. Consider offering to waive or reduce it in exchange for prompt payment of the $4,505 principal.
  • Partial Payments: After serving a 3-day notice, accept partial payments only with a compliant § 83.56(5) reservation to avoid waiving eviction rights.
  • Holdover Double Rent: If facts support holdover after lease expiration, consider asserting double rent under § 83.58 for the holdover period; weigh this against the goal of speedy resolution.
  • Mitigation and Reletting: While Florida law does not impose an explicit statutory duty to mitigate for residential leases, promptly marketing the unit supports reasonableness and reduces ongoing losses.
