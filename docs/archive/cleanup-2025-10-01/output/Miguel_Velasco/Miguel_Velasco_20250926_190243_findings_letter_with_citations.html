Governing Law and Legal Standards (Florida)

1) Duty to Disclose Material Latent Defects

  • Florida imposes on residential sellers a duty to disclose to buyers known facts materially affecting the value of the property that are not readily observable and not known to the buyer. (Johnson v. Davis, 480 So. 2d 625, 629 (Fla. 1985)).
  • This duty persists notwithstanding “as is” provisions or general disclaimers; such provisions do not shield a seller from liability for fraudulent nondisclosure or fraud in the inducement. (Johnson v. Davis, 480 So. 2d at 629; Mejia v. Jurich, 781 So. 2d 1175, 1178–79 (Fla. 3d DCA 2001)).

2) Fraudulent Misrepresentation / Fraud in the Inducement

  • Elements include: (a) false statement concerning a material fact; (b) knowledge that the statement is false; (c) intent that the statement induce another to act; and (d) injury in justifiable reliance. (Butler v. Yusem, 44 So. 3d 102, 105 (Fla. 2010); Besett v. Basnett, 389 So. 2d 995, 997–98 (Fla. 1980)).
  • A buyer may rely on a seller’s factual representations without conducting an independent investigation unless the falsity is obvious. (Besett, 389 So. 2d at 998).

3) Fraudulent Concealment / Failure to Disclose

  • Liability lies where the seller knows of a material defect or fact, has a duty to disclose it, and conceals or fails to disclose, inducing the buyer to act. (Johnson v. Davis, 480 So. 2d at 629; Billian v. Mobil Corp., 710 So. 2d 984, 987–88 (Fla. 4th DCA 1998)).

4) Negligent Misrepresentation

  • Florida adopts Restatement (Second) of Torts § 552: misrepresentation of a material fact made without reasonable care or competence, intended to induce reliance, causing damages to one who justifiably relies. (M/I Schottenstein Homes, Inc. v. Azam, 813 So. 2d 91, 94–95 (Fla. 2002)).

5) Broker/Agent Liability (if applicable)

  • Real estate brokers may be liable for misrepresentations or for failing to disclose known material facts. (Rayner v. Wise Realty Co. of Tallahassee, 504 So. 2d 1361, 1363–64 (Fla. 1st DCA 1987)).

6) Effect of Contract Clauses

  • Integration/merger clauses and “as is” provisions generally do not bar claims for fraud or fraudulent inducement. (Mejia, 781 So. 2d at 1178–79; Johnson v. Davis, 480 So. 2d at 629).
  • Florida’s economic loss rule does not bar well-pled intentional torts like fraud in the inducement. (Indem. Ins. Co. of N. Am. v. Am. Aviation, Inc., 891 So. 2d 532, 543 (Fla. 2004)).

7) Damages and Remedies

  • Rescission (equitable) may be sought where fraud or material nondisclosure induced the contract, subject to feasibility of restoring the status quo and absence of undue prejudice. (Johnson v. Davis, 480 So. 2d at 627–28).
  • Benefit-of-the-bargain damages for fraud: the difference between the property’s value as represented and its actual value at closing, plus consequential damages proven with reasonable certainty. (Ginsberg v. Lennar Fla. Holdings, Inc., 645 So. 2d 490, 495 (Fla. 3d DCA 1994)).
  • Cost of repair and/or diminution in value may be appropriate measures depending on the theory and proof; Florida recognizes diminution-in-value principles in real property defect contexts. (Grossman Holdings, Ltd. v. Hourihan, 414 So. 2d 1037, 1040–41 (Fla. 1982)).
  • Loss of use and related consequential damages may be recoverable where proximately caused and supported by evidence (e.g., alternative housing costs, lost rents).
  • Punitive damages may be available on a proper proffer for intentional misconduct or gross negligence, subject to statutory procedures. (Fla. Stat. § 768.72).
  • Attorney’s fees are generally not recoverable in tort absent a contract or statute. Many FAR/BAR forms contain prevailing-party fee provisions—contract review is necessary.

8) Limitations Periods and Accrual

  • Fraud/fraudulent inducement: four-year limitations period, running from the time the facts were discovered or should have been discovered with due diligence. (Fla. Stat. §§ 95.11(3)(j), 95.031(2)(a)).
  • Negligent misrepresentation: generally four years. (Fla. Stat. § 95.11(3)(a)).

9) Venue and Jurisdiction

  • Venue appears proper in Pinellas County; claims exceeding $50,000 would typically be filed in the Circuit Court of the Sixth Judicial Circuit.
  • Standard FAR/BAR contracts often require pre-suit mediation as a condition precedent to litigation—contract confirmation required.

Application to Your Matter

  • Seller Misrepresentation in Disclosure Forms: If the disclosure contains false statements about material conditions (e.g., prior water intrusion, roof, structural, mechanical, or permitting issues) and you relied on those statements in deciding to purchase or in setting the purchase price, you likely have viable claims for fraudulent and/or negligent misrepresentation. (Butler; Besett; Azam).
  • Failure to Disclose Latent Defects: If the seller knew of latent, material defects not readily observable and failed to disclose them, liability follows under Johnson v. Davis. The “as is” nature of a contract does not defeat such claims. (Johnson; Mejia).
  • Reliance and Investigation: Florida permits reliance on seller disclosures without independent verification unless the falsity was obvious. Your inspection history and any red flags will be relevant to the reliance analysis. (Besett).
  • Broker/Agent Exposure: If the listing or buyer’s agent knew or negligently conveyed inaccurate information, they may be joined for negligent misrepresentation or statutory/broker duties. (Rayner).

If substantiated, your damages may include: (i) cost to investigate and repair; (ii) diminution in market value if defects are irreparable or stigmatizing; and (iii) consequential losses such as loss of use, temporary housing, and lost rental value, as provable. (Ginsberg; Grossman).

Pleading Theories and Counts

  • Count I – Fraudulent Misrepresentation (false material statements in disclosure; knowledge; intent; justifiable reliance; damages). (Butler; Besett).
  • Count II – Fraudulent Concealment/Nondisclosure (known material latent defects; duty to disclose; failure to disclose; reliance; damages). (Johnson; Billian).
  • Count III – Negligent Misrepresentation (material misstatements made without reasonable care; justifiable reliance; damages). (Azam).
  • Count IV – Alternative Equitable Relief (rescission and restitution, if feasible), pled in the alternative to damages. (Johnson).
  • Additional Parties (if warranted): listing broker/agent (negligent misrepresentation), home inspector (negligence) based on findings.

Damages Framework

  • Benefit-of-the-bargain / Diminution in Value: Value as represented minus actual value at closing.
  • Cost to Repair: Labor, materials, permits, and related professional fees necessary to cure defects.
  • Consequential Damages: Loss of use (temporary housing, storage), lost rents, and incidental costs reasonably flowing from the defects.
  • Prejudgment Interest: As appropriate from the date of loss for liquidated components of damages.
  • Attorney’s Fees/Costs: If provided by contract or statute; otherwise costs taxable, fees by agreement or statute only.
  • Punitive Damages: Potential upon motion and evidentiary proffer under Fla. Stat. § 768.72 if intentional misconduct/gross negligence is demonstrated.

Procedural Considerations and Timing

Risks and Defenses to Anticipate

  • “As Is”/Merger Clauses: Do not bar fraud or nondisclosure claims, but may be argued to challenge reliance; our citations counter this. (Johnson; Mejia).
  • Buyer’s Inspection/Lack of Reliance: Defendants may argue defects were observable or that you did not rely; we will emphasize latent nature, seller knowledge, and your reliance on disclosures. (Besett).
  • Causation and Damages Proof: We must tie defects to pre-closing conditions and quantify losses with expert support.
  • Economic Loss Arguments: Not a bar to intentional torts; limited effect post–American Aviation. (891 So. 2d at 543).

Conclusion

Based on the information provided, Florida law affords strong avenues for relief where a seller misrepresents or fails to disclose known, material, latent defects. The viability of claims for fraudulent misrepresentation, fraudulent concealment, and negligent misrepresentation appears substantial, subject to evidentiary development.
