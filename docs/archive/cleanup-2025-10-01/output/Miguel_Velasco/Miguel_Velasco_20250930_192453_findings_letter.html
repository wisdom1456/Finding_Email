III. Governing Law and Key Authorities

  • Seller duty to disclose (common law): In Johnson v. Davis, 480 So. 2d 625 (Fla. 1985), the Florida Supreme Court held that a residential seller has a duty to disclose to a buyer any facts known to the seller that materially affect the value of the property and are not readily observable and not known to the buyer.
  • Reliance and buyer’s duty to investigate: Besett v. Basnett, 389 So. 2d 995 (Fla. 1980) recognizes that a buyer may rely on a seller’s material representations even if the buyer could have discovered the truth through investigation, unless the falsity is obvious or the buyer knows the statement is false.
  • Negligent misrepresentation: Gilchrist Timber Co. v. ITT Rayonier, Inc., 696 So. 2d 334 (Fla. 1997) outlines negligent misrepresentation and justifiable reliance in Florida.
  • “As Is” clauses: An “as is” contract does not eliminate the seller’s duty under Johnson v. Davis, nor the broker’s duty to disclose known material facts. See Syvrud v. Today Real Estate, Inc., 858 So. 2d 1125 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003); Rayner v. Wise Realty of Tallahassee, LLC, 967 So. 2d 436 (Fla. 1st DCA 2007).
  • Broker/agent duties: Florida brokers owe duties to disclose all known facts that materially affect the value of residential real property and are not readily observable to the buyer. See Fla. Stat. § 475.278.
  • Statutes of limitation:
    • Actions founded on fraud: four years. Fla. Stat. § 95.11(3)(j); accrues upon discovery. Fla. Stat. § 95.031(2)(a).
    • Negligence/negligent misrepresentation: generally four years. Fla. Stat. § 95.11(3)(a).
    • Breach of contract (if applicable): five years (written). Fla. Stat. § 95.11(2)(b).
  • Punitive damages (where warranted): Fla. Stat. § 768.72 (clear and convincing evidence of intentional misconduct or gross negligence required to plead punitive damages).
  • Disclosure carve-outs: No cause of action for failure to disclose property “stigma” (e.g., homicide, suicide, HIV). Fla. Stat. § 689.25.
  • Specific statutory disclosures: Radon notice requirement. Fla. Stat. § 404.056(5). Federal lead-based paint disclosures may apply to pre-1978 homes (42 U.S.C. § 4852d).
  • FDUTPA (optional/strategic): Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act, Fla. Stat. §§ 501.201–501.213, may apply in some real estate contexts and provides a fee-shifting mechanism (§ 501.2105), though its applicability to a single private sale depends on facts regarding “trade or commerce.”

V. Legal Analysis

A. Fraudulent Misrepresentation

  • Elements: (1) False statement of material fact; (2) knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard; (3) intent to induce reliance; (4) justifiable reliance; and (5) damages.
  • Application: If the seller knowingly falsified answers on the seller disclosure, element (1) and (2) are implicated. Your reliance is generally justified under Besett v. Basnett unless the falsity was obvious or you knew the truth.
  • Remedies: Benefit-of-the-bargain damages (value as represented minus actual value), cost to repair, consequential damages (e.g., temporary housing and loss of use), and potentially punitive damages where intent is proven under § 768.72.

B. Negligent Misrepresentation

  • Elements: (1) Misrepresentation of material fact believed to be true but made without reasonable care; (2) intent to induce reliance; (3) justifiable reliance; (4) damages.
  • Application: If the seller lacked knowledge but spoke without reasonable care regarding material facts, this claim may lie. Gilchrist Timber recognizes negligent misrepresentation without barring recovery for lack of exhaustive investigation by the buyer where reliance is otherwise justifiable.

C. Nondisclosure/Concealment under Johnson v. Davis

  • Elements: (1) Seller’s actual knowledge of facts; (2) facts materially affect value; (3) facts not readily observable and not known to buyer; (4) failure to disclose.
  • Application: If the undisclosed defects were latent (e.g., prior water intrusion, mold, structural issues) and the seller knew of them, liability may attach even with an “as is” contract.

D. Claims Against Real Estate Licensees (if involved)

  • Duty: Brokers must disclose all known facts that materially affect value and are not readily observable. Fla. Stat. § 475.278.
  • Application: If the listing or selling agent knew (or had reason to know) of defects and failed to disclose them, claims for negligent misrepresentation, statutory violations under Chapter 475, and aiding/abetting fraud may be viable.

E. Contractual Considerations

  • “As Is”/Merger/Disclaimer: These provisions typically do not bar claims for fraudulent inducement or nondisclosure. Florida courts allow such claims to proceed notwithstanding non-reliance language where intentional misconduct or nondisclosure is alleged.
  • ADR/Attorney’s Fees: Standard FAR/BAR residential contracts often require pre-suit mediation and contain prevailing-party attorney’s fee provisions. These may affect process and cost exposure. Please provide your purchase contract for review.

F. Damages

  • Diminution in Value: Difference between the value as represented and the actual value at sale, potentially supplemented by cost of repair where appropriate.
  • Cost of Repair: Reasonable and necessary repairs to remedy undisclosed defects (subject to proof of causation).
  • Consequential Damages: Loss of use (e.g., alternative housing or fair rental value), carrying costs during remediation (mortgage interest, taxes, insurance, HOA), and incidental expenses proximately caused by the misrepresentation/nondisclosure.
  • Punitive Damages: Available with clear and convincing evidence of intentional misconduct or gross negligence under § 768.72 (requires court leave to plead).
  • Attorney’s Fees/Costs: By contract or statute (e.g., FDUTPA § 501.2105), otherwise each party bears its own fees.

G. Potential Defenses and Risk Assessment

  • Readily Observable/Inspection Defense: Seller may argue the defects were patent or discoverable by a reasonable inspection. Under Johnson and Besett, buyer reliance remains viable for latent defects and for misstatements not obviously false.
  • Independent Inspector/“As Is” Clause: Use of an inspector and an “as is” contract does not absolve the seller or broker of duties for known latent defects; however, inspector findings may affect reliance and damages allocation, and may create separate claims against the inspector if negligence exists.
  • Merger/Non-Reliance Clauses: Typically do not bar fraud-based claims in Florida, but may be argued to limit reliance. Facts and contract language will matter.
  • Causation/Mitigation: Defendants may challenge whether claimed damages were caused by the alleged nondisclosure and whether you mitigated damages after discovery.
  • Stigma Exceptions: No duty to disclose certain psychological impacts (Fla. Stat. § 689.25).
