VI. Legal Analysis

A. Breach of Contract (Florida law)

  • Material breach occurs where performance is incomplete or defective, depriving the owner of the benefit of the bargain. The reported deficiencies and a bathroom requiring full redo support a claim of material breach.
  • Damages: Owner may recover the reasonable cost to repair and complete the work to the contract standard, plus consequential damages proximately caused by the breach (subject to contract limitations) and, where applicable, prejudgment interest and attorneys’ fees if provided by contract or statute.
  • Right to withhold payment commensurate with defective/incomplete work is generally recognized, particularly where statutory lien protections are followed.
  • Termination for cause may be available after notice and opportunity to cure in accordance with the contract and, for defect claims, with Chapter 558 pre-suit notice requirements. We will tailor the notice to satisfy both the contract and statutory prerequisites.

B. Construction Liens — Chapter 713, Florida Statutes

  • Overview: Subcontractors/suppliers not in direct contract with you (non-privity lienors) obtain lien rights by timely serving a Notice to Owner within 45 days after first furnishing labor/materials (§ 713.06(2)(a)), and by timely recording a Claim of Lien (generally within 90 days of last furnishing) (§ 713.08, § 713.22).
  • Notice of Commencement (NOC): If a NOC was recorded (§ 713.13), lienors rely upon it for project information. We will verify whether an NOC exists of record for your property and its dates.
  • Owner protections before payment:
    • Obtain contractor’s Final Payment Affidavit listing unpaid lienors before final payment (§ 713.06(3)(d)). You should demand this now to identify unpaid subs/suppliers.
    • Collect and condition any progress/final payments on conditional/unconditional lien waivers from the contractor and all known lienors (§ 713.20).
    • Use a Demand for Sworn Statement of Account from any lienor (§ 713.16(5)) to verify amounts owed, labor/materials furnished, and credits. Failure to respond timely can impair a lienor’s rights.
  • If a Claim of Lien is recorded:
    • You may discharge a lien by payment, by recording a Notice of Contest of Lien to shorten the foreclosure deadline to 60 days (§ 713.22(2)), or by transferring the lien to a bond or other security (§ 713.24) to free the property from encumbrance while disputes proceed.
    • We will evaluate whether to contest timeliness/validity (defects in service, amounts, description, exaggeration).
  • Statutory disclosures: Residential construction contracts exceeding $2,500 must include the § 713.015 owner’s lien law warning. We will review your contract for compliance; while omission does not necessarily eliminate lien rights, it is relevant to risk disclosures and contractor practices.

C. Failure to Pay Subcontractors and Owner Remedies

  • If you paid LLW without receiving proper waivers/releases, subs may still pursue liens. Timely use of § 713.16 and § 713.06 tools can mitigate “double payment” risk and build defenses.
  • Consider joint checks or escrow for any further payments to ensure funds reach unpaid subs if work continues under a cure plan.
  • We will demand proof of payments to subcontractors/suppliers, including ledgers, cancelled checks, and releases for all amounts covered by your $100,000 in payments.

D. Licensing and Disciplinary Exposure (Chapter 489; DBPR/CILB)

  • Grounds for discipline include, among others, abandonment of a project, deviation from plans/specifications, negligence resulting in significant harm, and financial mismanagement (§ 489.129).
  • If LLW or its qualifying agent violated licensing laws or engaged in misconduct, you may file a complaint with DBPR/CILB. Administrative remedies can support settlement leverage and public accountability.
  • Unlicensed contracting is prohibited (§ 489.127) and bars enforcement of the contract (§ 489.128), but our current information suggests LLW is licensed; we will verify licensure status and scope.

E. Consumer Protection (FDUTPA, §§ 501.201–501.213)

  • A deceptive or unfair act or practice in trade or commerce can support a civil action for actual damages (§ 501.211), plus attorneys’ fees to the prevailing party (§ 501.2105).
  • Alleged misrepresentations regarding materials (e.g., whether cabinets were purchased/stored with your funds) and marketing promises about quality/completion timelines may support FDUTPA claims if substantiated.

F. Potential Misapplication of Construction Funds / Fraud

  • Misapplication of funds: § 713.345 addresses misapplication of construction funds. If owner payments earmarked for specific labor/materials were diverted, this may be actionable and relevant to licensing discipline or referral.
  • Civil theft: § 772.11 provides a civil remedy (with pre-suit notice) for theft-related conduct under § 812.014; this is a serious allegation requiring clear evidence of felonious intent. We reserve this claim pending documentation.
  • We will seek documentation of the status of all materials purportedly purchased with your funds (e.g., cabinet invoices, delivery tickets, storage receipts, photographic proof).

G. Pre-Suit Construction Defect Procedures (Chapter 558)

  • For residential construction defect claims, Chapter 558 generally requires a written Notice of Claim describing defects and allowing inspection and opportunity to cure before filing suit. The notice period is typically 60 days (see § 558.004).
  • We will structure the default/cure letter to comply with both your contract and Chapter 558 to preserve claims and avoid premature filing defenses.

H. Attorneys’ Fees and Costs

  • Review your contract for a unilateral or prevailing-party fee provision. Some statutory claims (e.g., FDUTPA § 501.2105; lien actions § 713.29) allow fee shifting.
  • Fee exposure is a critical factor in settlement strategy and litigation risk assessment for both sides.
