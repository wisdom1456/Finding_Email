V. Analysis

A. Material Alteration of Common Areas Contrary to the CCRs

  • Governing standard. While language varies, “material alteration” is generally understood as a substantial change to the appearance, function, or use of common elements that would be noticeable to a reasonable observer and not merely routine maintenance. Illustrative authority recognizes that such changes require the level of owner approval specified in the governing documents (e.g., Sterling Village Condominium, Inc. v. Breitenbach, 251 So.2d 685 (Fla. 4th DCA 1971) (defining material alteration); see also Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes §6.10, §6.13).
  • Application. Replacing constructed boardwalks with loose pea gravel significantly changes the surface’s nature, function, and safety profile. This is not routine maintenance but a substantial change to the common area’s character and usability. On the present facts, it fits the commonly applied “material alteration” standard.
  • CCR compliance. If the CCRs require a 75% community vote for material alterations, the Board’s unilateral action is ultra vires and voidable or void. Courts generally enforce CCR-imposed voting thresholds for such changes (Nahrstedt v. Lakeside Village, 8 Cal.4th 361 (1994) (recorded restrictions enforced if reasonable); Hidden Harbour Estates, Inc. v. Basso, 393 So.2d 637 (Fla. 4th DCA 1981)).
  • Remedies. Declaratory and injunctive relief to: (i) declare the action unauthorized; (ii) enjoin further noncompliant changes; and (iii) require restoration of the status quo ante (reinstallation of boardwalks) pending a properly noticed vote.

B. Fiduciary Duties; Business Judgment Rule Limits; Governance Procedures

  • Duties owed. HOA directors owe duties of care, loyalty, and good faith, and must act within the scope of the governing documents (Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes §6.13). The business judgment rule does not shield decisions that contravene the CCRs or exceed delegated authority (Lamden v. La Jolla Shores Clubdominium HOA, 21 Cal.4th 249 (1999); Levandusky v. One Fifth Ave., 75 N.Y.2d 530 (1990)).
  • Procedural compliance. Material alterations typically require: (i) notice and agenda disclosure; (ii) owner vote if mandated; (iii) compliance with development orders and permits; and (iv) documented deliberation of safety impacts and alternatives. The Board’s failure to obtain the required vote and disregard of an 80% petition weighs against deference under any business judgment standard.
  • Exposure. Potential claims include breach of governing documents, breach of fiduciary duty, and waste/mismanagement of common assets (if reinstallation becomes necessary due to unauthorized removal).

C. Violation of Development Order and Local Code

  • Binding effect. A duly issued development order is enforceable under local code until amended by the issuing authority. Private actors may not unilaterally deviate from its conditions.
  • County violation. The county’s issuance of a violation corroborates noncompliance. Continued noncompliance can trigger fines, corrective directives, or, in some jurisdictions, liens or stop-work orders.
  • Interplay with CCRs. Even if the county were to amend the development order, private CCR restrictions remain independently enforceable among owners unless lawfully amended by the required owner vote. A county approval does not override private covenants.

D. Public Safety and Nuisance Concerns

  • Safety hazards. Loose pea gravel can increase slip/ankle injury risk, impede mobility devices, and degrade pedestrian accessibility. The Board’s duty to maintain common areas in a reasonably safe condition is widely recognized.
  • Nuisance exposure. Replacing stable boardwalks with gravel may substantially interfere with the comfortable use and enjoyment of property for certain owners, supporting claims under general nuisance principles (see Restatement (Second) of Torts §§821D–F).

E. Attempt to Amend Development Order Without Owner Approval

  • County process vs. private rights. The Board may apply to the county to amend a development order; however, the HOA cannot use administrative amendments to evade the CCRs’ voting requirements. Courts routinely hold that compliance with public approvals does not excuse violation of private covenants.
  • Relief pathways. Owners can pursue: (i) administrative opposition before the county (written objections, hearing testimony); and (ii) civil injunctive relief preventing the HOA from implementing changes that contravene the CCRs absent the required vote—regardless of county action.

F. Failure to Comply With County Enforcement

  • If the HOA has not promptly cured the violation, owners can request stepped-up enforcement by the county (compliance deadlines, fines), and may seek court orders compelling the HOA to comply with both public requirements and private covenants.

VI. Risk Assessment

  • For the HOA/Board: Court-ordered restoration costs; fines for code noncompliance; attorneys’ fees (many CCRs/statutes are fee-shifting); potential D&O insurance claims and increased premiums; exposure to safety-related claims.
  • For Owners if no action: Ongoing safety risks; diminished use/enjoyment; potential property value impacts; reduced leverage if the county amends the development order first (though CCR enforcement would still remain available).

VIII. Urgency and Procedural Options

Time‑Sensitive: The county application deadline noted as August 16 requires prompt administrative objections and requests for continuance if needed. Parallel preparation for judicial relief is advised if the Board proceeds without owner approval.

A. Internal HOA Remedies

  • Owner demand and cure letter. Demand the Board: (i) cease further implementation; (ii) notice and conduct the required 75% vote; and (iii) restore the boardwalks or adopt a temporary safety plan pending a vote.
  • Records inspection. Invoke statutory and CCR inspection rights to obtain the documents listed above.
  • Special meeting and vote/recall. If permitted by the CCRs/statute, notice a special meeting to address restoration and, if necessary, initiate a director recall for noncompliance.
  • ADR requirements. Some jurisdictions require pre‑suit mediation/arbitration for HOA disputes. We will confirm and comply to preserve fee recovery and injunctive options.

B. Administrative (County) Remedies

  • File written objection referencing the development order’s original intent and safety rationale; attach the 80% petition and photographs.
  • Request staff denial or continuance pending an HOA owner vote to ensure the county is not used to bypass private covenants.
  • Attend and testify at any hearing; submit expert statements on safety/accessibility and covenant compliance.
  • Enforcement follow‑up urging compliance deadlines and escalating sanctions if the Board persists.

C. Judicial Remedies

  • Temporary Restraining Order/Preliminary Injunction. To halt further alteration, block implementation of any county amendment, and require restoration pending final adjudication, based on likelihood of success (CCR violation), irreparable harm (safety and loss of covenant rights), and balance of equities/public interest.
  • Declaratory judgment. Establish that the Board’s actions violate the CCRs and that the required 75% vote applies to any material alteration; declare that county administrative changes do not excuse CCR compliance.
  • Breach of fiduciary duty/waste. If supported by evidence, to recover corrective costs and fees.
