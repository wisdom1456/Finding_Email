Findings Letter and Strategic Recommendations

I. Executive Summary

Based on the facts provided, the HOA Board’s removal of the existing boardwalks on Salt Box Lane and replacement with pea gravel—contrary to the applicable community development order and without the 75% membership approval required by the Covenants, Conditions & Restrictions (CCRs)—likely constitutes: (1) an unauthorized, material alteration of common areas in breach of the CCRs; (2) a violation of the community development order and county land‑use/code enforcement requirements; and (3) a potential breach of the Board’s fiduciary duties to the membership. The Board’s effort to seek a post‑hoc amendment to the development order, while ignoring a petition signed by approximately 80% of Salt Box Lane homeowners, further heightens legal exposure and public‑safety risk.

Immediate goals are: restore the original boardwalks, halt or defeat the unilateral development order amendment, and enforce the CCRs and development order. We recommend swift, parallel action: formal demand and records requests to the HOA; support of county enforcement; pursuit of pre‑suit mediation as required for HOA disputes while preparing for emergency injunctive relief; and engagement with the county’s administrative process to oppose any amendment that would legitimize the removal without proper owner approval.

II. Conflicts Check

III. Factual Background

  • The community development order required boardwalks on Salt Box Lane. The HOA Board removed those boardwalks and installed pea gravel.
  • The CCRs prohibit material alterations of common areas without a 75% community vote. No such vote occurred.
  • County authorities issued a violation for the unauthorized removal.
  • Rather than restore the boardwalks, the Board seeks to amend the development order, without homeowner approval.
  • A petition signed by approximately 80% of Salt Box Lane homeowners requesting reinstatement of the boardwalks was submitted and ignored by the Board.
  • The change has created safety and accessibility issues, including forcing pedestrians into the roadway.

IV. Governing Law and Frameworks

  • CCRs and HOA Governing Documents: Contractual covenants control common‑area alterations and approval thresholds (here, 75% membership for material alterations).
  • Community Development Order: Binding local land‑use approval governing site improvements (including pedestrian facilities) and enforced by county code enforcement.
  • HOA Fiduciary Duties: Directors owe duties of care and loyalty to the Association and must act within the authority of governing documents (see Florida Statutes Chapter 720, including Section 720.303 regarding association powers and duties), if Florida law applies.
  • County Land‑Use/Code Enforcement: Local regulations and enforcement mechanisms (commonly implemented under Florida Statutes Chapter 162 in Florida jurisdictions) authorize investigations, hearings, and per‑day fines for violations.
  • Safety/Accessibility: General premises‑liability principles and, where applicable, federal disability laws such as the Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)) and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) may require reasonable modifications/accommodations to common areas when requested by persons with disabilities, depending on the nature of the facility and access.

Note: Jurisdiction is unspecified. Citations above reference Florida law as a prevalent framework for HOAs and county land‑use enforcement. If another state governs, equivalent statutes and local codes will apply.

V. Legal Issues and Analysis

A. Unauthorized Material Alteration of Common Areas (CCRs)

  • Issue: Whether removal of the boardwalks and installation of pea gravel is a “material alteration” requiring a 75% membership vote under the CCRs.
  • Analysis: The substitution of a constructed, stable pedestrian facility with loose aggregate fundamentally changes safety, accessibility, durability, and aesthetics. This is a classic material alteration. Because the CCRs expressly require a 75% vote for material alterations, the Board’s unilateral action exceeds its authority and is ultra vires.
  • Consequence: The alteration is voidable and subject to injunctive relief compelling restoration and compliance. Attorney’s fees may be available to the prevailing party enforcing governing documents (see, e.g., Florida Statutes § 720.305 in Florida jurisdictions).

B. Breach of Fiduciary Duties by the HOA Board

  • Issue: Whether the Board breached its duties of care and loyalty by acting contrary to the CCRs, ignoring member input, and increasing risk/liability.
  • Analysis: Fiduciary duties require adherence to governing documents and prudent risk management (see, e.g., Florida Statutes § 720.303). Acting without required owner approval, disregarding an 80% petition, and creating known safety hazards suggest conduct outside the business‑judgment rule. Post‑hoc attempts to change external approvals rather than cure violations further evidence potential bad faith or gross negligence.
  • Consequence: Members may seek injunctive/declaratory relief against the Association and, where appropriate, claims against directors for breach of duty. D&O insurance notice is advisable.

C. Violation of Community Development Order and County Code

  • Issue: The county issued a violation for unauthorized removal of required pedestrian improvements.
  • Analysis: The Board’s action conflicts with the development order. Local code enforcement, often operating under Florida Statutes Chapter 162, can impose per‑day fines, liens, and orders to cure. A later amendment application does not erase the underlying violation or the CCR breach; it is an attempt to retroactively legalize a noncompliant condition.
  • Consequence: Continued noncompliance exposes the Association to escalating fines, enforcement hearings, and potential liens until restoration or authorized modification is approved following proper procedures.

D. Attempt to Amend Development Order Without Homeowner Approval

  • Issue: Whether pursuing a development order amendment can bypass CCR voting requirements.
  • Analysis: External approvals (e.g., county development orders) and internal covenants are independent. Even if the county amended the development order, the CCRs would still require member approval for material alterations. The Board cannot use a county amendment to sidestep CCR voter thresholds or to retroactively validate an ultra vires act.
  • Consequence: Members may seek declaratory relief that any amendment does not authorize CCR violations and may enjoin implementation absent required owner approval.

E. Public Safety and Accessibility

  • Issue: Whether replacing boardwalks with pea gravel creates unreasonable hazards and potential accessibility barriers.
  • Analysis: Forcing pedestrians into the roadway and removing a stable surface presents foreseeable risks of injury and property‑value impacts. Where residents have mobility impairments, the Association may be obliged to consider reasonable modifications/accommodations (Fair Housing Act, 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f); ADA applicability depends on whether the facility is a place of public accommodation or otherwise covered).
  • Consequence: Elevated exposure to negligence and discrimination claims. Interim safety measures and accessible alternatives should be implemented immediately pending full restoration.

F. Procedural Requirements for Dispute Resolution

  • Issue: Pre‑suit steps typically required for HOA disputes.
  • Analysis: In Florida, many HOA disputes must proceed through pre‑suit mediation (see Florida Statutes § 720.311). Courts may entertain requests for temporary injunctive relief to prevent irreparable harm while parties pursue required alternative dispute resolution. We will tailor to the governing jurisdiction’s procedures if outside Florida.

  • Member Harm: Reduced safety and accessibility; diminished property values; governance drift if ultra vires actions are normalized.

VIII. Recommendations and Action Plan

A. Immediate (0–2 Weeks)

  • Formal Demand to Board: Demand immediate reinstatement of the boardwalks and cessation of all actions to amend the development order absent required owner approval. Cite CCR provisions, county violation, and fiduciary duties. Reference remedies and attorneys’ fees (e.g., Florida Statutes § 720.305).
  • Records Request: Submit a written request for association records (see, e.g., Florida Statutes § 720.303(4)), including all contracts, minutes, communications, legal opinions, and filings relating to the removal and proposed amendment.
  • Safety Mitigation: Demand interim measures (temporary stabilized pathways, cones/signage, lighting) and engagement of a licensed engineer or accessibility consultant.
  • County Engagement: Provide a homeowner statement supporting the violation, request continued enforcement and compliance deadlines, and ask to be notified of all hearings. Seek to intervene or appear as affected parties in any amendment proceeding.
  • Preservation Notice: Send a litigation hold to the Board and vendors to preserve evidence (emails, texts, plans, bids).
  • D&O Insurance Notice: Provide notice to the Association’s D&O carrier to facilitate coverage for corrective action and defense costs.
  • Accessibility Requests: Where applicable, submit or support reasonable accommodation/modification requests for residents with disabilities under the Fair Housing Act.

B. Short Term (2–6 Weeks)

  • Pre‑Suit Mediation: Serve the statutory pre‑suit mediation notice if Florida law applies (Florida Statutes § 720.311) or the equivalent in the governing jurisdiction, seeking a mediated agreement to restore the boardwalks and withdraw the amendment application.
  • Prepare for Emergency Relief: Draft a verified complaint for declaratory and injunctive relief and a motion for temporary injunction to preserve the status quo and require restoration. Grounds include breach of CCRs (material alteration without vote), violation of fiduciary duties, and ongoing code noncompliance.
  • County Process: File written objections and expert reports with the county; argue that amending the development order would undermine approved pedestrian infrastructure and is inconsistent with the original approval. If Florida law applies, evaluate remedies under Florida Statutes § 163.3215 to challenge development orders inconsistent with the comprehensive plan.
  • Owner Mobilization: Organize signatures beyond the 80% petition and consider calling a special meeting to direct the Board to restore facilities, or initiate director recall if warranted (see, e.g., Florida Statutes § 720.303(10)).

C. Litigation Track (As Needed)

  • Counts: Enforcement of CCRs; declaratory relief; injunctive relief; breach of fiduciary duty; negligence for unsafe conditions; and, as appropriate, claims tied to code violations. Seek attorneys’ fees as allowed by statute or contract.
  • Relief Sought: Order compelling reinstatement of the boardwalks; prohibiting implementation of any development order amendment absent required owner vote; and compliance with county directives.
  • Coordination with County: Request that the court consider county enforcement status and orders; a court injunction can complement administrative enforcement.

D. Governance and Long‑Term Compliance

  • Policy Reforms: Adopt a written “Material Alterations” policy tied to CCR thresholds; require engineering review and member vote for any common‑area change affecting safety or accessibility.
  • Board Training: Annual fiduciary‑duty and open‑records compliance training (see Florida Statutes § 720.303 for guidance in Florida).
  • Capital Planning: Implement a reserve and maintenance plan for pedestrian infrastructure aligned with development orders and local codes.
