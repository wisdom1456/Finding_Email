Executive Summary

This letter summarizes the factual findings, identifies the primary legal issues, and sets forth recommended immediate and longer-term actions to achieve the clients' objectives. The HOA Board removed the existing boardwalks on Salt Box Lane and replaced them with pea gravel, creating reportedly unsafe walking conditions that have forced pedestrians into the street [1]. The county issued a violation for the removal of the boardwalks [1]. The Board is pursuing an amendment to the development order rather than restoring the boardwalks, reportedly without homeowner approval, and a neighborhood petition with approximately eighty percent signatures demanding reinstatement was ignored [1]. The community CCRs require a 75% community vote for material alterations of common areas, a requirement the Board appears not to have followed [1]. The urgency level of this matter is critical to address safety concerns and to prevent unauthorized changes to common areas [1].

Identified Legal Issues

  • Whether the Board's removal of the boardwalks and installation of pea gravel constitutes an unauthorized material alteration of common areas contrary to the CCRs [1].
  • Whether the Board's actions violate the applicable county development order and any applicable local land-use or permitting requirements, as reflected by the county violation [1].
  • Whether the Board breached its fiduciary duties by failing to follow CCR-required procedures for material alterations, ignoring the neighborhood petition, or otherwise acting without proper homeowner approval [1].
  • Whether any attempted amendment to the development order can be used to retroactively authorize the change without homeowner consent or in contravention of the CCRs [1].
  • Immediate public-safety and nuisance concerns stemming from the changed walking conditions [1].

Legal Analysis (Summary)

Based on the assembled facts, several legal theories and enforcement pathways merit simultaneous pursuit.

  • Contractual / CCR Enforcement: The CCRs, which set binding obligations and approval thresholds for material changes to common areas, are the primary contractual instrument through which homeowners may challenge unauthorized alterations. If the CCRs require a 75% community vote for material alterations, the Board’s removal and replacement likely exceeds its unilateral authority without obtaining the required homeowner approval [1].
  • Administrative / Land-Use Enforcement: The county issued a violation for the removal, indicating potential noncompliance with the community development order and county regulations. County enforcement mechanisms (e.g., fines, mandatory restoration orders, stop-work orders) may be available to compel restoration of the boardwalks or otherwise require compliance with the development order [1].
  • Fiduciary Duty and Governance Claims: HOA directors owe fiduciary duties of loyalty and care to the association and its members. Unilateral material alterations without observing CCR amendment or voting procedures, and ignoring a substantial neighborhood petition, may support claims that the Board breached its fiduciary duties [1].
  • Injunctive Relief and Emergency Remedies: Given the reported safety hazard that is forcing pedestrians into the roadway, plaintiffs may be able to secure expedited injunctive relief or a temporary restraining order to require immediate mitigation (e.g., restoration, interim safe walking surfaces, signage, or traffic-calming measures) pending final resolution [1].

Immediate Risks

  • Risk to public safety from unsafe pedestrian conditions that may cause injury and potential liability claims for the HOA [1].
  • Risk of the Board obtaining an amended development order (or attempting to) that seeks to validate the unauthorized change absent proper homeowner approval [1].
  • Risk of precedent for future unauthorized alterations if the Board succeeds in avoiding enforcement or homeowner input [1].

Recommended Actions

Given the critical urgency and the clients' priorities, I recommend the following immediate and staged actions.

Immediate (within 48–72 hours)

  • Demand letter to the HOA Board and management: Send a formal demand requiring immediate temporary mitigation to protect pedestrian safety (e.g., temporary boardwalks, clear signage, barricades, restoration to pre-existing surface), and require cessation of any efforts to amend the development order until homeowner vote and regulatory compliance are confirmed [1].
  • Notify county enforcement authority: Provide the county with documented evidence of the Board’s actions and request immediate enforcement action or clarification of the county’s intent following its issuance of a violation [1].
  • Plead for emergency relief: Prepare documents to seek temporary injunctive relief or emergency orders from the appropriate court to require immediate corrective measures to eliminate the safety hazard if the Board does not comply promptly [1].

Short Term (2–6 weeks)

  • Demand restoration of the original boardwalks as required by the development order and CCRs, subject to county permitting and approval processes if necessary [1].
  • Preserve evidence: Collect and preserve photographic evidence, communications from the Board, HOA meeting minutes, copies of the CCRs and development order, and the neighborhood petition with signatures [1].
  • Seek declaratory relief: File a complaint seeking declaratory relief that the Board’s action violated the CCRs and any applicable development order, and seek injunctive relief requiring restoration [1].

Long Term

  • Governance overhaul and compliance monitoring: Seek court-ordered or negotiated governance remedies to ensure the Board follows CCR amendment procedures and that any future changes to common areas follow proper notice, voting, and permitting processes [1].
  • Damages and equitable relief: Assess claims for equitable relief and, if appropriate, damages for diminution of property values or other losses attributable to the Board’s actions [1].

The plaintiffs’ principal priorities are to obtain reinstatement of the original boardwalks on Salt Box Lane, prevent amendment of the development order without homeowner approval, ensure the Board complies with the CCRs and its fiduciary duties, remove the immediate safety hazard to pedestrians, and preserve neighborhood aesthetics and property values [1]. These are the objectives we will pursue in parallel through administrative and judicial remedies [1].

The factual record indicates that the Board removed and replaced boardwalks on Salt Box Lane with pea gravel, creating immediate safety hazards, and did so without adherence to the CCR voting thresholds and apparently without homeowner consent; the county has issued a violation and the Board is attempting to amend the development order rather than restore the boardwalks, while ignoring a neighborhood petition with about eighty percent support for reinstatement [1]. Immediate legal and administrative steps are warranted to restore the boardwalks, protect pedestrian safety, prevent unauthorized retroactive approvals, and enforce the CCRs and development order.
