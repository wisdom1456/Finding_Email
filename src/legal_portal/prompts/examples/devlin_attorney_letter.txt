Subject: Legal Review and Recommended Next Steps – LLW Construction, Inc. Dispute

Good afternoon Mr. Devlin and Ms. Bell,

I hope you are doing well. I wanted to follow up with a summary of our findings after reviewing the documents you submitted, including the contract with LLW Construction, Inc. and the Notice to Owner, regarding your property at 3414 South Belcher Drive, Tampa, Florida 33629.

As discussed, the primary concern is whether you can hold the contractor responsible for the incomplete work and how to protect your home from potential liens by unpaid subcontractors.

Based on our review, we understand that the issues began after you engaged LLW Construction, Inc. to complete repairs to your home following damage from Hurricane Helene. The total contract amount was $128,355.77, of which $100,000 has already been paid. Despite this substantial payment, the contractor has not completed the project, and the work performed has been substandard—including serious construction defects such as improper bathroom framing that required you to reconstruct it yourself. Since January, the contractor's only communications have been requests for more money, despite minimal progress. Additionally, you received a Notice to Owner from a subcontractor for over $3,000, indicating they were not paid by the contractor for their work.

Here are the key points of our analysis:

• Under Florida law, there's an important protection called an "implied warranty of workmanlike construction." This means that contractors are required to perform their work competently, even if your contract doesn't explicitly state this. In your case, LLW Construction violated this protection by leaving the bathroom framing incomplete and ceasing communication after receiving substantial payments. What this means for you: you have a strong legal basis to demand that they either complete the work properly or reimburse you for hiring another contractor to finish the job.

• Before you can pursue legal action against a contractor in Florida, you must follow a process outlined in Florida Statutes Chapter 558. This statute requires you to send a formal notice to the contractor detailing the defects in their work, giving them 60 days to respond with a plan to remedy the issues. If they propose a repair plan, they have an additional 45 days to complete the repairs. This process is mandatory and designed to encourage resolution without court intervention. For you, this means we need to initiate this notice process before considering litigation.

• A breach of contract occurs when one party fails to fulfill their obligations under the contract terms. LLW Construction's failure to complete your home repairs despite receiving $100,000 constitutes a clear breach. What this means for you: you may be entitled to monetary damages, the cost of hiring a replacement contractor, and reimbursement for any subcontractor payments you've had to make.

• You received a Notice to Owner from a subcontractor, indicating they have not been paid. In Florida, even if you have paid the general contractor, you can still be liable for unpaid subcontractors—this is called "lien exposure." This notice is the first step toward filing a construction lien against your property. If unresolved, the subcontractor can file a lien, which is a legal claim against your property that must be settled before you can sell or refinance. In the worst-case scenario, it could lead to the forced sale of your home. However, this is preventable if we act quickly.

Based on the above, a negotiated resolution would likely be your most efficient and cost-effective path forward. One option would be for our firm to send a formal demand letter to LLW Construction on your behalf, requiring them to complete the project according to the contract terms or reimburse you for the costs of hiring another contractor. This letter would also provide the required 60-day notice under Chapter 558 and seek reimbursement for payments you've made or will make to subcontractors.

Additionally, you should consider paying the subcontractor directly (approximately $3,000+) to avoid a lien being placed on your home. You can then seek reimbursement of this amount from LLW Construction as part of your damages claim.

If you decide to proceed with payment to the subcontractor, here's what you need to do to protect yourself:

• Obtain a lien waiver in writing – This document confirms the subcontractor has been paid and relinquishes their right to place a lien on your property.
• Keep proof of payment – Save the cancelled check, receipt, or signed acknowledgment for your records.
• Document this as part of your damages – Keep records so we can include this amount when seeking reimbursement from LLW Construction.

Please let us know if you would like us to proceed with drafting and sending the above-referenced demand letter, or whether you would prefer that we first set a phone call to discuss our review and recommendations for next steps.
