# FORMATTING RULES - NATURAL FLOW STYLE

## CRITICAL: Letters should flow naturally like professional correspondence, NOT like legal memos with rigid section headers.

```
Subject: Legal Review and Recommended Next Steps – [Case Name]

Good afternoon [Client Name],

I hope you are doing well. I wanted to follow up with a summary of our findings after reviewing [specific documents], including [list key docs], regarding your property at [address].

As discussed, the primary concern is [state main issue in plain English].

[2-3 paragraphs describing the situation - what happened, when, current status]

Here are the key points of our analysis:

• [Legal concept explained in plain English—what it means]. [How it applies to your situation]. [What this means for your options].

• [Next legal point explained plainly]. [Application to facts]. [Practical impact].

• [Additional points as needed, each as a complete paragraph without bold headers]

Based on the above, a negotiated resolution would likely be your most efficient path forward. [Specific recommendation with timeline]. [Additional steps if needed].

[Protective checklist if client needs to take action - with explanations]

Please let us know if you would like us to proceed with [specific action], or whether you would prefer that we first set a phone call to discuss.

Thank you,

[Attorney Name]
[Title]

This analysis is based on the documents provided and represents a preliminary assessment. Additional facts may emerge that will materially affect this analysis. This communication is confidential and subject to attorney-client privilege.
```

---

## KEY FORMATTING PRINCIPLES

### 1. Natural Flow - No Rigid Headers
The letter should read like professional correspondence, NOT like a legal memo with formal section numbers.

**FORBIDDEN:**
- "1. FACTUAL SUMMARY" (too formal)
- "RECOMMENDED ACTION & NEXT STEPS" (too formal)
- "Key Findings" (too formal)
- Bold issue titles like "**Implied Warranty**:" 

**REQUIRED:**
- Natural paragraph flow
- "Here are the key points of our analysis:" as transition
- Each bullet is a complete paragraph explaining the concept

### 2. Plain Language Throughout
Every legal term must be explained in plain English:

✅ GOOD:
"Under Florida law, there's a protection called an 'implied warranty'—this means contractors are legally required to do competent work, even if your contract doesn't say so."

❌ BAD:
"You may have claims under the implied warranty of workmanlike construction."

### 3. Bullet Format - Complete Paragraphs Without Bold Headers

**CORRECT:**
```
Here are the key points of our analysis:

• Under Florida law, there's an important protection called an "implied warranty of workmanlike construction." This means contractors are legally required to do competent work, even if your contract doesn't specifically say so. In your case, LLW Construction violated this protection by leaving the bathroom incomplete. What this means for you: you have a strong legal basis to demand they either finish properly or pay for someone else to do it.

• Before you can sue a contractor in Florida, you must follow a process under Chapter 558. Think of it as a required 'cool-down period.' You send a formal notice describing what's wrong, they get 60 days to respond with a repair plan, then 45 days to fix it. You cannot skip this—Florida law requires it before any lawsuit.

• You received a Notice to Owner from a subcontractor—this is a warning they haven't been paid. Here's why this matters: even if you paid the general contractor, you can still be liable for unpaid subs. If unresolved, they can place a lien on your property, and in the worst case, force a FORCED SALE OF YOUR HOME to collect.
```

**WRONG:**
```
• **Implied Warranty & Construction Defects (Florida Statutes Chapter 558)**: An "implied warranty" is...
```
(This has a bold header - we don't want that)

### 4. Protective Checklists With Explanations

When recommending client action, explain WHY each step matters:

```
If you decide to pay the subcontractor, here's what you need to do:

• Get a lien waiver in writing – This confirms they've been paid and gives up their right to place a lien. Don't pay without this.

• Keep proof of payment – Save the cancelled check or receipt. You'll need this if there's any dispute.

• Document this for your damages claim – You shouldn't have had to pay directly. Keep records so we can seek reimbursement from the contractor.
```

## COMPLETE WORKING EXAMPLE (Natural Flow Style)

```
Subject: Legal Review and Recommended Next Steps – LLW Construction Dispute

Good afternoon Mr. Devlin and Ms. Bell,

I hope you are doing well. I wanted to follow up with a summary of our findings after reviewing the documents you submitted, including the construction contract with LLW Construction Inc., payment records, and the Notice to Owner, regarding your property at 3414 South Belcher Drive, Tampa, Florida.

As discussed, the primary concern is whether you can hold the contractor responsible for the incomplete work and how to protect your home from potential liens by unpaid subcontractors.

Based on our review, we understand that the issues began after you engaged LLW Construction Inc. to rebuild your home for a total contract price of $128,000. To date, you have paid $100,000, but the contractor has only completed work valued at $70,000—a $30,000 shortfall. Work stopped in March 2025—over 8 months ago—and you haven't heard from them since despite multiple attempts to reach them.

Additionally, you received a Notice to Owner from Tibbetts Lumber for $2,150.66. This is a formal warning that they haven't been paid for materials supplied to your project.

Here are the key points of our analysis:

• Under Florida law, there's an important protection called an "implied warranty of workmanlike construction." This means contractors are legally required to do competent work, even if your contract doesn't specifically say so. In your case, LLW Construction violated this protection by leaving the bathroom framing incomplete and ceasing all communication after receiving $100,000. What this means for you: you have a strong legal basis to demand they either complete the job properly or pay for someone else to do it.

• Before you can sue a contractor in Florida, you must follow a specific process under Florida Statutes Chapter 558. Think of it as a required "cool-down period" where both sides get a chance to resolve things without going to court. Here's how it works: you send the contractor a formal written notice describing exactly what's wrong, and they get 60 days to respond with a repair plan. If they respond, they get another 45 days to actually fix the problems. You cannot skip this step—Florida law requires it before any lawsuit. For you, this means we need to send this notice before we can take stronger legal action.

• You received a Notice to Owner from Tibbetts Lumber—this is a formal warning that they haven't been paid for materials. Here's why this matters to you directly: in Florida, even if you already paid the general contractor, you can still be held responsible for unpaid subcontractors and suppliers. If this isn't resolved within 90 days of their last delivery, Tibbetts can place a lien on your property—a legal claim that must be paid before you can sell or refinance your home. In the worst case, they can force a FORCED SALE OF YOUR HOME to collect what they're owed. This is serious, but it's also preventable if we act now.

Based on the above, a negotiated resolution would likely be your most efficient and cost-effective path forward. I recommend two immediate actions: (1) Pay Tibbetts Lumber the $2,150.66 and obtain a lien waiver to protect your property, and (2) Send a Chapter 558 pre-suit notice to LLW Construction demanding they complete the work or reimburse you.

If you decide to pay Tibbetts Lumber, here's what you need to do to protect yourself:

• Get a lien waiver in writing – This is a document confirming they've been paid and giving up their right to place a lien on your property. Don't pay without getting this first.

• Keep proof of payment – Save the cancelled check, receipt, or signed acknowledgment. You'll need this if there's any dispute later.

• Document this as part of your damages – You shouldn't have had to pay this directly (your contract was with LLW Construction). Keep records so we can include this amount when seeking reimbursement from the contractor.

Please let us know if you would like us to proceed with drafting the Chapter 558 notice and demand letter, or whether you would prefer that we first set a phone call to discuss these recommendations.

Thank you,

Franklin Riley
Senior Partner

This analysis is based on the documents provided and represents a preliminary assessment. Additional facts may emerge that will materially affect this analysis. This communication is confidential and subject to attorney-client privilege.
```

## COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

### ❌ MISTAKE 1: Formal Section Headers
```
WRONG:
1. FACTUAL SUMMARY
[content]
2. RECOMMENDED ACTION & NEXT STEPS
```

```
CORRECT:
[Natural paragraph flow without formal headers]
Based on our review...
Here are the key points of our analysis:
• [bullet paragraphs]
Based on the above...
```

### ❌ MISTAKE 2: Bold Issue Titles
```
WRONG:
• **Implied Warranty & Construction Defects (Florida Statutes Chapter 558)**: An "implied warranty" is...
```

```
CORRECT:
• Under Florida law, there's an important protection called an "implied warranty of workmanlike construction." This means...
```

### ❌ MISTAKE 3: Legal Jargon Without Explanation
```
WRONG:
Pursuant to Florida Statutes § 713.06, the subcontractor has lien rights that may result in foreclosure.
```

```
CORRECT:
You received a Notice to Owner—this is a warning that a subcontractor hasn't been paid. Here's why this matters: even if you paid the general contractor, you can still be liable. If unresolved, they can place a lien on your property, and in the worst case, force a sale of your home.
```

## QUALITY CHECKLIST

Before finalizing, verify:

### Plain Language
- [ ] Every legal term is explained in plain English
- [ ] "Why this matters" is clear for each point
- [ ] No unexplained jargon

### Natural Flow
- [ ] NO formal section headers (FACTUAL SUMMARY, RECOMMENDED ACTION)
- [ ] NO bold issue titles in bullets
- [ ] Letter reads like professional correspondence
- [ ] "Here are the key points of our analysis:" introduces legal points

### Content Quality
- [ ] Warm greeting (Good morning/afternoon preferred)
- [ ] Specific documents and property address mentioned
- [ ] Each bullet is a complete paragraph
- [ ] Protective checklists explain WHY each step matters
- [ ] Clear call to action at end

### Self-Test
- [ ] Would someone without legal training understand this?
- [ ] Does it feel like a letter from a caring professional?
